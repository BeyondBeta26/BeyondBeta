Layer 7 · (European) system of laws and values

“It Is Not Hard to Build an App Store”

10 min read20 Nov 2026

The Digital Markets Act as a tool for European innovation and competitiveness: an interview with Fiona Scott Morton, Yale School of Management

The Friday 13th Microsoft outage once again showed how much depends for Europe and European companies on competitive digital markets and on having alternatives available. For the #BeyondBeta team, the timing was almost a little ironic, as we had just discussed exactly this topic with one of the most influential thinkers in the field. Fiona Scott Morton is an icon of the economics of competition policy and antitrust enforcement. She is Professor of Economics at the Yale School of Management and an Adjunct Professor at Yale Law School and the most prominent voice globally on, among other things, the European Digital Markets Act. Her perspective could not be timelier and more relevant to tech sovereignty: shaping digital markets is not only about reining in "the big ones". It is just as much a driver of competitiveness and innovation.

But first, a short intro to set the scene: The Digital Markets Act (DMA), which has been in force for four years this November, is one of the EU's strongest cards in the geopolitics of tech. At its core, the DMA is a competition instrument that requires tech companies designated as "gatekeepers" of large digital platforms to open up parts of their business and make space for competitors. For example, they must allow third parties to interoperate with their services in specific contexts. A practical example: iMessage must be interoperable with other messaging services. The DMA also prohibits gatekeepers from treating their own products and services more favourably than similar ones offered by rivals. A practical example of that we all have noticed: Google can no longer give preference to Google Maps when it comes to finding directions. As a result, Maps is now a few clicks away instead of front and centre, if you search for an address on Google. The aim is to give consumers more choice in digital services and better control over their personal data. European and US businesses, in turn, should benefit from access to data, distribution channels and greater interoperability. So far, the European Commission has designated Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, Booking, ByteDance, Meta and Microsoft as gatekeepers, and it is now moving to extend the DMA to the cloud services AWS and Microsoft Azure.

If designated gatekeepers fail to comply with the DMA, they can be fined up to 10% of their global annual turnover, and further remedies, both financial and non-financial, are possible. A hefty punishment, in theory. So far, Apple, Meta and Google have been fined for breaching the DMA, although the fines of €500 million, €200 million and €890 million respectively stayed far below the maximum. More recently, the Commission ordered Google to share anonymised search data with rival search engines so they can improve their products. Google is challenging that decision in court. Meanwhile, Apple will not launch Siri AI on iPhones and iPads in the EU. Under the DMA, it would have to give rival AI assistants the same system access that Siri gets, and Apple argues that such third-party access, even when the user starts it, would be a security risk.

The US government in particular criticises the DMA for targeting its Big Tech companies. The EU pushes back on this narrative, saying the DMA's objective is to ensure compliance rather than to issue fines. Yet while this is a first step, it overlooks a crucial element: European innovation does not appear simply because US competitors comply. The DMA is not a complete solution, but it creates the opportunity to unlock that innovative potential.

How exactly? And what else does it take? Read on for our deep dive with Professor Scott Morton!

Why Do European Businesses Not See the DMA as an Opportunity?

Kirsten Rulf: The DMA is probably the sharpest sword in Europe's toolbox. In public, it is mostly used to restrict the power of the big US tech companies. Your work looks at it from a different angle: as a force that can unlock competitiveness and innovation in Europe. Why is it so hard for European businesses to understand that the DMA is not about punishing American companies, but about opening markets for them?

Fiona Scott Morton: There are three reasons. One is that the tech companies each have a large and sophisticated communications department, and they push this narrative.

Second, politicians often measure success by how many tech companies a country has within its borders, rather than by whether people are productive, have good employment opportunities and can use their skills. If you think the first way, the natural reflex is to keep rivals out so that domestic firms can grow, but the second approach invites them in while requiring a level playing field.

And the third is a certain amount of learned helplessness. If European firms spend decades building on technology that comes from the US without nurturing their own skills, own venture capital, etc., then the risk is that the innovation muscle atrophies and people just sit there waiting for something to happen.

Kirsten Rulf: That matches our experience. Some large European media houses would have the reach to make their own app store a success. Yet when you put it to them, there is very little appetite.

Fiona Scott Morton: And it is not hard to build an app store. It is not frontier technology; other people have done it. You can hire the tech lead from any one of them and say: build me an app store. Put your own products in it and give everyone a discount for using it. Then you get users' payment methods, business users can ask consumers to share data. All kinds of things become totally reasonable and easy.

Who Should Be Using the DMA Right Now?

Kirsten Rulf: If you had to name the European companies that most need to use the DMA as what it was actually meant to be, a freedom tool, who would they be?

Fiona Scott Morton: That is hard to say, because the DMA has so many parts that it affects different industries differently. On the advertising side, the big media groups, of course. On the technology side, there is a whole group of app store players.

Then there is hardware, all the wearables. And there is AI. Right now, the two dominant mobile operating systems, Android and iOS, are locked down for rival AI assistants. You cannot get the functionalities users expect: looking at the screen, the microphone, the camera, the wake word, access to apps. Those APIs are not shared on either platform.

Whose Job Is It to Explain the DMA?

Kirsten Rulf: Maybe that is part of the problem: people do not go through the provisions of the DMA, just as they do not really understand the AI Act or the DSA. Is it the Commission that does not explain these laws and is not close enough to business? Or are businesses simply not making the effort?

Fiona Scott Morton: I do not think the Commission thinks it is its job to explain the DMA to business users, which is interesting. And the politicians are busy talking about how it will impede US companies, not about how it will help local ones.

Martijn Snoep and I held a conference on innovation and the DMA in June 2024 in Schiphol. We brought together entrepreneurs from across the EU, from digital advertising, app stores and other areas, to talk about the innovation they were going to undertake because the DMA existed. Margrethe Vestager, then Executive Vice-President of the Commission, opened the day, and the whole conference was very uplifting. Everybody was excited. Not every company felt comfortable presenting on stage, but many of them came anyway and shared their experiences in conversations on the sidelines.

What Can the DMA Contribute to Sovereignty?

Kirsten Rulf: What could the DMA contribute to sovereignty?

Fiona Scott Morton: Once you have your own businesses at home inventing and building things, these are businesses that operate under European law, and the people running them live in Europe and are subject to European law. That makes things much better.

Second, a general regime of interoperability gives you more choice: more entry, and no single provider everyone depends on. Say you have a US firm, a Canadian firm, a Turkish firm and a South Korean firm, but no European firm. If there is a sovereignty or security concern, you can tell them what you need. Because users could switch to another provider, each provider has a strong incentive to find a solution. Of course, there will be regulatory pushback about timelines and details, which is normal. In the end, though, providers in a competitive market want to keep their customers, so they work with the regulator.

What Would Real Enforcement Look Like?

Hanna Mrugalla: How can we strengthen enforcement? Is it a matter of pace, fines or scope?

Fiona Scott Morton: Take app stores. Apple has allowed rival app stores, but it charges high fees and insists on taking fees on transactions it did not contribute to at all. Initially, it had a programme that explicitly taxed users who used rival app stores, to discourage entry. Then it moved to taxing the rival app stores themselves at a very high rate; now the rate is lower. The Commission does not approve of this and is in dialogue with the gatekeeper. But if it does not in the end impose some kind of penalty for noncompliance, there is no reason for a company to comply.

And there is a legal risk in waiting. When the Commission eventually does act, the company can say: we have been charging this fee for two years, we thought we were compliant. If you thought this was illegal, why did you not use the process under the DMA? And I hope a court does not find that plausible because it understands the pressure from the Trump administration, but you never know.

Real enforcement would mean opening a non-compliance proceeding right away. And if it becomes the third non-compliance finding within eight years, the DMA allows the Commission to go as far as structural remedies and merger bans. At that point, a gatekeeper might think: that sounds serious, maybe we should try to achieve compliance.

Another example is AI. If a platform has to open its operating system to rival AI assistants, it matters whether its own assistant is then given privileged access elsewhere, for example inside the platform's other apps. That is exactly the kind of situation the DMA's anti-circumvention rules are designed for.

Has Europe Given Up on AI and Cloud Too Early?

Kirsten Rulf: You said earlier that Europe almost seems afraid of its own innovation. Could the DMA help reverse that? Could startups, Mistral but also much smaller ones, use it far more proactively with their clients?

Fiona Scott Morton: First, it looks to me like the foundation models are not going to yield financial returns. There are three big ones, plus a couple of challengers chasing them, so maybe five. If we can sustain competition, and that is a big if, they will become commodities and will not be able to sustain sufficient margins, then these would not be businesses that Europeans are sorry to have skipped. What foundation models are working on now are capabilities for very difficult things: folding proteins, designing rockets, solving really hard math problems. There is limited demand for that level of service.

What is worth worrying about is how to maintain competition and access to all those past generations of models. That is very much a sovereignty question. Everybody's harness has to be able to switch out the model it uses, and there has to be a way to store open-weight models physically in Europe, with their parameters, so that Europe is not dependent on infrastructure outside its own jurisdiction. But I do not think that is an innovation issue.

On cloud, having capacity in Europe is very important. But there are multiple ways to get there. One option would be regulation rather than subsidies: define clear security requirements for where European business and personal data may be stored and set a date from which they apply. Demand for providers that meet those standards would follow. You do not have to tax anyone; you do not need public money. The Commission would have to define what constitutes safe, so that providers can build compliant services. Existing providers could adapt their structures to comply. This is a fairly straightforward solution.

The remaining problem, however, is somewhat intractable: agglomeration. Look at an economy the size of the United States. There is one place for designing cars, one or two for movies, one or two tech hubs, one for oil and gas, one for textiles. If you want to start an agglomeration with public money, that money cannot be split into 27 pots for 27 different attempts, one in each member state. They will not be scaled properly, and it will not work. But if there can only be one agglomeration for a given technology in Europe, that is a problem because several member states want it. And I do not know what the solution to that is, because, understandably, no member state wants to see an entire industry concentrated somewhere else.

Is CADA Aiming at the Right Target?

Kirsten Rulf: The Cloud and AI Development Act is exactly the kind of tool you are describing, one that separates the data you can put on a hyperscaler from the data you cannot. But again, we see a lobbying battle in Brussels.

Fiona Scott Morton: Of course. And that covers only the public sector, by the way. So, governments are burdened with a high price and inefficient service. It would be far better to name the problem everyone actually sees, which is the private economy. If access to essential digital services or payments can be cut off from outside, that is a sovereignty threat. It is not about public data. It is about the entire economy.

And you would lessen the lobbying if the requirements for Amazon, Microsoft and Google to meet the EU's security concerns were strict but doable. Obviously, they have to be effective. But if they are, those companies would simply comply. They want to sell. It is a market of 450 million people with high purchasing power.
